\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

VanniKawachh is a public safety system centered on an autonomous drone designed to reach reported incidents, provide an aerial view, and record visual evidence for responders. It responds only to hub verified distress alerts. At a verified location, the aircraft can clarify conditions and support a faster, better informed response. Its flight control system receives a target location and manages the journey, observation, and return phases. The drone is the response unit, while the sensing node supplies the initial alert.

The fixed sensing node listens for possible distress sounds, including screams, cries, or calls for help. It screens the audio and sends a candidate alert with its registered location to a local hub. The hub checks the available audio and context before requesting dispatch. This separation helps ensure that a single sound classification does not directly trigger the aircraft.

Together, the sensing node, verification hub, and drone form a connected response chain. The node identifies a possible event, the hub evaluates it, and the drone travels to the site to gather visual information. VanniKawachh aims to bring aerial response to incidents where conventional communication may be limited. Detection accuracy, communication reliability, and flight safety require controlled testing before operational use.

\vspace{0.5cm}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} women's safety; acoustic event detection;
edge inference; audio verification; LoRa; authenticated reporting;
drone response; software-in-the-loop.

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

Consider where an assault actually happens. It is rarely on a
well-lit arterial road under a CCTV camera. It is on the dark stretch
between two streetlights, on the shortcut through a wooded campus
edge, or in the far corner of a parking area at 23:40. These locations
share three properties. First, they are surveillance dead
zones: no camera, no patrol, often marginal or absent cellular
coverage. Second, they are response dead zones: even a
successful emergency call produces a ground unit minutes away through
traffic. Third, and this is the property existing technology ignores,
they are places where the victim is least able to operate a device:
hands occupied, phone snatched, discharged, or simply out of reach in
the seconds that matter.

The market's answer has been to instrument the victim. Panic-button
mobile apps, smart wearables, GPS pendants, and IoT panic devices all
place the trigger on the person at risk. The best of these report
high accuracies, but the failure mode is structural: a safety
device the victim must carry, charge, and reach protects only the
moments in which she can carry, charge, and reach it. The research
community's alternative, audio surveillance systems that detect
screams, has demonstrated strong detection rates, but on server-scale
compute, and it stops at detection. The output is a classifier score,
not a located alert or a response on the ground.

What is missing is infrastructure that owns the whole chain:
sensing that asks nothing of the victim, verification that suppresses
false alarms well enough to act on, alert delivery that works
precisely where cellular does not, and a first response that arrives
in seconds rather than minutes. VanniKawachh is a design, an
implementation, and a staged validation of that chain.

\section{The inverted-burden principle}
\label{sec:inverted}

VanniKawachh's single organizing idea is that the burden of triggering
help must move from the victim to the environment. A scream, a cry, or
a shouted ``help'' / ``bachao'' is a signal the victim produces
anyway, under any circumstance, with no device. If pole-mounted
infrastructure can hear it, verify it, and act on it, then the
victim's participation requirement drops to zero: no app, no
wearable, no phone, no button.

The engineering consequences of that idea drive every design decision
in this thesis:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Nodes must be cheap, solar, and everywhere.} So the
  node is an ESP32-S3 class microcontroller with a TinyML Stage-1
  model rather than a streaming link to a server.
\item \textbf{False alarms must be suppressed before anything
  expensive happens.} A drone launch is costly and
  attention-consuming, so a second, heavier verification stage runs
  at a hub, fused with environmental evidence.
\item \textbf{The alert path must not depend on cellular coverage.}
  The target locations are dead zones by definition, so the alert
  travels over LoRa.
\item \textbf{An alert that launches a drone must be unforgeable.} So
  every packet is encrypted, authenticated, and replay-protected.
\item \textbf{The response aircraft must be trusted to fly
  unattended.} So the response layer is a flight stack whose safety
  properties are pinned by automated tests, built and validated
  before this concept existed (v1 of this project) and carried over
  unchanged.
\end{itemize}

\section{Problem statement}
\label{sec:problem}

Design and implement a distributed safety network in which
pole-mounted acoustic nodes detect distress audio on-device, a
locality hub confirms each event with deep audio analysis fused with
environmental evidence, confirmed alerts travel encrypted over LoRa
with the node's surveyed coordinates to a police-facing dashboard, and
an autonomous quadcopter is dispatched to the incident to record
evidence during a guarded observation window, such that (i)~no continuous
audio leaves any node; (ii)~no alert can be spoofed or replayed into a
drone launch; (iii)~no dispatch occurs below explicit verification and
severity thresholds; (iv)~every flight-mode command is confirmed from
autopilot telemetry, no mission can start against an airborne vehicle,
and hazard responses obey debounce, severity, and escalation
semantics; and (v)~each of these properties is demonstrated by
automated, reproducible tests before it is claimed.

\section{Scope}
\label{sec:scope}

In scope: the three-tier architecture; the node firmware structure and
its Stage-1 pipeline; the complete hub service (packet cryptography,
registry, Stage-2 verification, evidence fusion, dispatch gating); the
response flight stack and its evidence-recording extension; controlled
Stage-1 and hub-model benchmarks; end-to-end validation in
software-in-the-loop simulation; and the physical hardware inventory
and field-test gates. Out of scope for the prototype, and stated as
such wherever relevant: hardware range, latency, endurance and
field-false-alarm measurements; live video streaming to police, multi-node
time-difference localization, and beyond-visual-line-of-sight flight
operations.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item An \textbf{end-to-end architecture} (infrastructure sensing
  $\rightarrow$ two-stage verification $\rightarrow$ offline encrypted
  alerting $\rightarrow$ autonomous field response) where prior
  work covers at most one link of the chain
  (Chapters~\ref{ch:literature} and~\ref{ch:design}).
\item A \textbf{two-stage edge/hub acoustic pipeline}: a recall-tuned
  MFCC + CNN screen on the node ($<$50~ms budget) and a
  precision-tuned PANNs + sensor-fusion confirmation on the hub, with
  explicit thresholds gating dispatch (Chapter~\ref{ch:node-hub}).
\item A \textbf{secure LoRa alert protocol}: a 25-byte sealed packet
  (AES-128-CTR + truncated HMAC-SHA256 + per-node monotonic replay
  counter, per-node keys derived from a master key) carrying an event
  class rather than coordinates, resolved against a surveyed node
  registry at the hub (Chapter~\ref{ch:node-hub},
  Appendix~\ref{app:packet}).
\item A \textbf{safety-verified autonomous response layer} inherited
  from the group's v1 flight stack (verified mode transition,
  landing interlock, failsafe arbitration), extended with an
  evidence-recording window during hover
  (Chapter~\ref{ch:response}).
\item A \textbf{staged validation record}: an earlier 68-case
  software-test inventory, an 8/8 SITL acceptance flight, and a
  zero-hardware rehearsal (Phase~0). These are historical software
  results with heuristic stand-ins labelled as such
  (Chapter~\ref{ch:testing}).
\end{enumerate}

\collegechapter{Literature Review}
\label{ch:literature}

\section{Survey method}
\label{sec:surveymethod}

The group's literature survey (twelve papers, 2023 to 2026, first
catalogued in the Title Finalization Seminar record and reproduced
with full bibliographic detail as references~\cite{pavithra}
to~\cite{ciaburro} of this thesis) covered the women-safety
technology space across two communities: consumer/IoT safety devices
and acoustic event detection. Every surveyed system falls into one of
two buckets, and the buckets split on a single question: who carries
the trigger?

\section{Bucket 1: victim-carried devices}
\label{sec:bucket1}

Mobile panic applications, smart wearables (bands, pendants, footwear
sensors), and standalone IoT panic buttons
\cite{pavithra,bharathi,snehith,hadkar,uganya,potturi} place the
sensing and triggering hardware on the victim. Pavithra et
al.~\cite{pavithra} present a discreet smartphone application with
on-device audio recognition and emergency automation; Bharathi et
al.~\cite{bharathi} describe an IoT wearable combining GPS and
accelerometer sensing and report \textbf{97.54\%} detection accuracy
with a 3.2~s response time and a 1.92\% false-positive rate; Snehith
et al.~\cite{snehith} and Hadkar et al.~\cite{hadkar} present
push-button/wearable IoT devices with locate-and-alert behaviour;
Uganya et al.~\cite{uganya} describe a button-triggered GSM/GPS
tracker; and Potturi et al.~\cite{potturi} automate the SOS trigger
with GSM/GPS geo-location sharing. The stronger systems combine
multiple modalities (accelerometer gestures, heart-rate anomalies,
voice keywords), and GPS positioning with GSM/app-based alerting is
near-universal in this bucket.

Availability remains an important issue alongside classifier
accuracy. A carried-device trigger may be inaccessible when the
device is absent, damaged, discharged, or out of reach. Its reported
accuracy does not measure this access failure. App and GSM alerting
also depend on coverage, so each proposed site needs a network
assessment \cite{uganya}.

\section{Bucket 2: detection-only audio systems}
\label{sec:bucket2}

The acoustic event detection literature
\cite{kimwindow,sharma,srimathi,kimscream,fime} demonstrates that
distress audio is machine-detectable at useful rates. Kim et
al.~\cite{kimscream} built an 11{,}921-sample large-scale scream
dataset and found a CNN-Transformer to be the best of five evaluated
models; the same group~\cite{kimwindow} later added a windowing CNN
for scream temporal-interval prediction, improving F-measure by
roughly three percentage points and equal-error rate by an order of
magnitude. Sharma and Jebaseeli~\cite{sharma} report a
scream-and-panic detector at \textbf{92\%} accuracy with under 5\%
false positives; Fime et al.~\cite{fime} achieve \textbf{95.51\%}
danger-sound accuracy with Noisereduce preprocessing and
transfer-learned image backbones (InceptionV3, with MobileNetV2 as a
lighter alternative) over mel-spectrogram inputs; and Srimathi et
al.~\cite{srimathi} show that scream detection can reduce reaction
time by 40\% and increase intervention rates by 30\%. Ciaburro and
Puyana-Romero's systematic review of sound-event detection in smart
cities~\cite{ciaburro} confirms both the maturity of detection methods
and the near-absence of closed-loop response systems in the
literature.

Three limitations matter for this project. First, the compute and
power budget of a fixed solar node differ from those in larger-model
experiments \cite{kimwindow,kimscream}. Second, results from curated
recordings do not predict an outdoor microphone's false alarms.
Third, many studies evaluate classification without also measuring
authenticated location reporting and a guarded physical response.

\section{The gap}
\label{sec:gap}

Overlaying the buckets exposes the gap this project fills. Bucket~1
has response paths (alerts to guardians/police) but burdens the
victim. Bucket~2 removes the victim's burden but has no response path.
\textbf{No surveyed system provides end-to-end infrastructure sensing
$\rightarrow$ verified offline alerting $\rightarrow$ autonomous field
response.} That chain, with each link individually feasible on
published evidence, is VanniKawachh's contribution, and each link
imports a discipline from a different literature: TinyML keyword
spotting for
the node~\cite{tflm}, large-scale pretrained audio tagging
(PANNs~\cite{panns}) for the hub, LPWAN practice for the alert
path~\cite{lorawan,sx1278}, and the group's own SITL-verified dispatch
stack for the
response~\cite{ardupilot,dronekit,pymavlink,dronekitsitl}.

\section{Technology base}
\label{sec:techbase}

\textbf{TinyML on microcontrollers.} TensorFlow Lite
Micro~\cite{tflm} supports quantized models on microcontrollers.
Its example pipelines inform the proposed MFCC-fronted node model,
but target-board RAM use and timing must be measured on the actual
ESP32-S3 configuration.

\textbf{PANNs.} Pretrained Audio Neural Networks~\cite{panns} (CNN14
and lighter variants trained on AudioSet) produce class scores
over hundreds of sound events, including the
distress-relevant family (screaming, shouting, crying, yelling,
wailing). Combining relevant classes yields a candidate distress
score, but its threshold still needs evaluation on target audio.
This makes PANNs an option for Stage-2
verifier on a Raspberry Pi~5.

\textbf{LoRa.} Semtech SX1278-class radios~\cite{sx1278} deliver
low-rate links with no cellular operator (the LoRaWAN
specification~\cite{lorawan} documents regional parameters). Actual
range and throughput depend on frequency, settings, and site. The
nominal data rate is enough
enough for a 25-byte alert and far too little for audio, which shapes
the split transport design of Section~\ref{sec:rationale}.

\textbf{The autopilot stack.} ArduPilot and MAVLink~\cite{ardupilot}
and software-in-the-loop simulation~\cite{dronekitsitl} are covered in
the group's v1 work; Chapter~\ref{ch:response} summarizes what carries
over. The v1 finding that motivates the whole project's verification
philosophy bears repeating: the standard client idiom for changing
flight mode was observed to fail silently on ArduCopter 3.3
SITL: the library reports success while the autopilot ignores the
command. The design answer, a detection claimed is not a
detection confirmed; a command sent is not a command adopted, now
governs every layer of VanniKawachh: sound, dispatch, and flight mode
alike.

\textbf{Regulation.} India's Drone Rules, 2021
(G.S.R.~589(E))~\cite{dronerules} govern the prototype's flight
operations (registration, zone constraints, visual line of sight); the
Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023~\cite{dpdp} and plain
privacy prudence govern the audio design; and the Wireless Planning
and Coordination Wing's delicensing framework~\cite{wpc} governs the
radio links. Chapter~\ref{ch:compliance} consolidates all three.

\section{Personal safety systems: what they solve and what they assume}

Personal safety applications address a practical need: a person should be able to contact trusted people or emergency services without having to remember a long sequence of steps. The systems surveyed in this work approach that goal through a phone application, a wearable device, or a dedicated button. Their implementations differ, but the common workflow is recognizable: the user activates a trigger, the device packages an alert and some location information, and a network path carries the message to a contact or service. Pavithra et al. explore audio recognition and automated actions within a phone application, while Potturi et al. describe automatic alerts and location sharing using GPS and GSM \cite{pavithra,potturi}. These systems reduce the number of manual operations required after activation.

They do not, however, remove the dependency on the phone being present, charged, accessible, and connected. Wearable approaches add sensors that can recognize a gesture or infer a possible emergency. Bharathi et al. discuss a design combining location, movement, audio, and physiological sensing; Snehith et al. and Hadkar et al. describe IoT safety devices; and Uganya et al. present a GPS/GSM tracker with a manual trigger \cite{bharathi,snehith,hadkar,uganya}. Each arrangement reflects a reasonable product choice: sensing on the person can follow the user, and a personal device can be configured with the user's contacts. It also creates a maintenance obligation. The user must remember to carry, charge, and keep the device functional. A user may also be unable to operate it during a rapidly developing event.

This is not a flaw unique to one paper; it is a design boundary shared by systems whose alarm begins on a personal device. Reported performance values in these papers should be read in the context of their specific datasets, sensors, test conditions, and definitions of an emergency. A classifier's accuracy does not measure whether a user is wearing the device, whether GPS has a useful fix, or whether an alert reaches a responder. Similarly, a short measured alert delay in a controlled prototype does not establish a guaranteed response time in a different network or city. For the present project, the relevant lesson is therefore not that a fixed public node will always outperform a wearable.

Rather, the two architectures cover different conditions. A carried device follows its owner. A fixed node may help at a known location without requiring the person to activate equipment. VanniKawachh investigates the latter setting and keeps personal devices outside the required trigger path.

\section{Acoustic event detection and the meaning of a positive result}

Sound-event recognition offers a way to detect a possible emergency without waiting for a button press. Its appeal is also its main challenge: many ordinary events can resemble distress in a short recording. A loud vehicle, children playing, a sports call, a musical sound, or a clipped microphone signal may all produce a strong classifier response. A system must therefore be evaluated not only on whether it recognizes selected positive examples, but also on how it behaves across long periods of ordinary background audio. The studies by Kim et al. \cite{kimwindow} develop scream recognition with attention to noisy conditions and temporal localization. Their later CNN–Transformer and Windowing CNN system models local spectral patterns and temporal context, and predicts the interval associated with a scream rather than returning only a clip-level decision.

This is relevant to an always-listening node because the location in time of an event can guide which short segment should be preserved for review. The earlier large-scale scream-dataset work provides a complementary dataset and model-evaluation perspective \cite{kimscream}. These contributions show why a distress system needs to consider both classification and the event's temporal boundaries. Sharma and Jebaseeli examine scream and panic recognition as a safety application, while Srimathi et al. consider how detection can be connected to an alerting workflow \cite{sharma,srimathi}. Fime et al. study danger-sound classification with preprocessing and deep-learning methods, including approaches designed for noisy recordings \cite{fime}. Across these papers, the choice of label set, recording source, train/test partition, and noise construction affects the meaning of the resulting score.

A model trained to distinguish a particular scream dataset from selected negatives is not thereby validated for every street, microphone, language, distance, or acoustic reverberation. This distinction matters for VanniKawachh. The system's first classifier is a screening stage: a positive result means that a short window should be passed to a second decision, not that an incident has been proven. The hub's Stage-2 score and contextual inputs are separate evidence, and a policy gate must still be met before a mission request is created. Conversely, a negative screening result can miss a quiet or distant event, so the screening model's recall and microphone coverage remain important field questions. The thesis reports the current held-out dataset metrics as model measurements and does not reinterpret them as an outdoor false-alarm rate.

\section{Noise, distance, and temporal evidence}

Real outdoor sound differs from a clean training clip in several ways at once. The microphone may be far from the speaker; a wall or vehicle may obstruct the path; the source may move; and wind, traffic, crowd sound, or electrical noise may mask parts of the signal. Reverberation can stretch a short vocal event, while automatic gain control or clipping can change its amplitude profile. These effects can change the features supplied to a model even when the person produces the same word or scream. For that reason, a single spectrum or fixed frequency threshold is not a dependable distress rule. The noisy-condition experiments in Kim et al. are useful because they treat noise as part of the evaluation setup and consider the localization of the scream interval \cite{kimwindow}.

Fime et al. likewise examine preprocessing and classification in danger-sound recognition \cite{fime}. These studies support evaluation across distinct acoustic conditions, but their reported results remain tied to their own mixtures, sensors, and test protocols. A noise type represented in a dataset is not equivalent to every field combination of distance, microphone placement, weather, and background activity. Temporal evidence can help distinguish a sustained background from a short human event, but it too is context-dependent. The Stage-1 front end in this project frames audio and computes MFCC features so the compact model can compare changes across successive windows. The hub can examine a longer event clip and associate its score with the initial screening confidence. The design therefore uses the waveform as a sequence, not as a single instantaneous loudness value.

Motion, illumination, and time-of-day provide additional context, but none of these measurements proves distress on its own. A PIR sensor may respond to benign movement; a low light reading may simply describe night-time conditions; and the hour is only a weak prior. The fusion stage is intended to combine these imperfect cues while retaining an independent acoustic threshold. The literature suggests two practical evaluation layers. Laboratory tests should vary signal-to-noise ratio, source distance, and event duration under repeatable conditions. Field tests should then record representative background periods and report false alarms per unit of monitoring time, missed events, end-to-end latency, and operating range. The second layer is necessary because clip-level classification metrics alone do not capture how often a continuously listening installation would raise an alert in daily use.

VanniKawachh has not yet completed that field campaign; it is listed as future validation, not inferred from the held-out clip score.

\section{Edge inference and the division of computation}

An infrastructure microphone could send all audio to a central computer, but continuous streaming increases bandwidth use, power demand, network dependence, and privacy exposure. A small model at the microphone offers a different arrangement: analyse short frames locally and transmit only when a candidate event is found. TensorFlow Lite Micro provides an implementation path for running quantized neural models on microcontrollers with constrained memory \cite{tflm}. The review by Ciaburro and Puyana-Romero surveys sound-event detection in smart cities and the trade-offs involved in processing acoustic data \cite{ciaburro}. The trade-off is between model capacity and operational cost. A small quantized model can run close to the microphone, but it may have less representational capacity than a larger model.

A central model can examine more context, but it requires an available link and enough processing resources. The VanniKawachh architecture assigns the low-cost task to the ESP32-S3 and the more detailed review to the hub. This division also supports a useful safety boundary: the edge device can request review but cannot authorize the aircraft. Should a model update, feature-extraction change, or runtime problem occur, the effect can be isolated and tested at the stage where it belongs. The same division affects measurement. Model size is only one resource metric. A field-ready edge implementation also needs its inference latency, peak memory, power draw, thermal behavior, and watchdog/recovery behavior measured on the exact board and firmware build.

A desktop benchmark cannot substitute for those measurements. Likewise, a model that fits in flash may still be too slow for the intended frame cadence. The current thesis reports the deployed model's size and the separate held-out classification result, while explicitly listing board-level energy and timing measurements as incomplete. Pretrained audio models provide another way to allocate computation. PANNs use largescale audio pretraining and include CNN-based architectures that produce scores for multiple sound classes \cite{panns}. Such a model can provide a second opinion using a broader vocabulary than a tiny screening network. It does not eliminate the need to validate the target labels, calibrate scores, or test the specific Raspberry Pi runtime. A high distress-related class score can still occur in a non-emergency sound, so the hub's role is verification under a policy, not unquestioned acceptance of a pretrained model's output.

\section{Communication and location in a distributed installation}

Emergency reporting has two different communication needs. The system must send a small, urgent message with the identity of the reporting device and enough information to retrieve the event. It may also need a larger audio segment for verification. A single low-rate radio link is poorly suited to both tasks. LoRa has been investigated for emergency-management situations where ordinary mobile infrastructure may be absent \cite{sciullo,lorawan}. Its low data rate is compatible with a compact alert, but makes it a poor choice for transferring an audio clip. This motivates the split path in VanniKawachh: a short LoRa packet reports the event and a separate local wireless link carries the bounded clip when available. The separation creates its own failure modes.

The LoRa alert may arrive while the audio link is unavailable, or the clip may arrive after the verification window has expired. The receiver must associate both messages with the same event, reject stale or mismatched clips, and record when the verification evidence was absent. Treating a missing clip as if it were a successful Stage-2 classification would hide uncertainty. The system instead records the alert with Stage-2 confirmation unavailable and does not dispatch. Radio range, packet delivery rate, interference, antenna orientation, and latency must be measured at the proposed installation site. Protocol documentation alone does not establish coverage for a particular pole or neighborhood. Location is also a system property rather than merely a GPS reading.

A fixed sensor can be surveyed at installation and registered against a node identifier. This avoids requiring a fresh coordinate fix for every alert, but makes correct installation records and registry integrity important. The node identifier must map to the correct installed coordinates, and a maintenance process is needed if hardware is moved or replaced. A mobile GPS/GSM tracker and a fixed node registry therefore have different error sources. VanniKawachh's design uses the surveyed location as the incident origin and treats GPS availability on the aircraft as a separate flight readiness condition. Security must cover message authenticity and freshness as well as radio delivery. Encryption without authentication does not prove that an event came from a registered node, and a previously valid alert should not be accepted repeatedly as a new incident.

The packet design therefore includes an authentication tag and a monotonic event counter, with the gateway kept as a forwarder rather than a dispatch authority. AES and HMAC are standard building blocks for confidentiality and integrity, respectively \cite{fips197,rfc2104}. Implementation choices such as key provisioning, counter persistence, tag length, and recovery after power loss still require careful treatment. Chapter 4 gives the implemented packet format and the specific limitations of its compact tag.

\section{Aerial response: useful capability with a higher safety burden}

An aerial vehicle can reach a location without following the same road path as a ground responder, and its camera can record conditions at the incident site. These properties have motivated research into drone-assisted emergency response. For the present thesis, the important point is not to assume that an aircraft can replace emergency services. A drone has a limited battery, weather and airspace constraints, and its own risks around people and buildings. It can only be considered as a supplementary response platform whose mission is bounded and whose operator retains a safe way to intervene. The control chain creates a different class of failure from acoustic classification. A correctly detected event can still lead to an unsafe outcome if a target coordinate is wrong, an autopilot mode change is not accepted, the vehicle has a stale position estimate, the battery is insufficient, or a previous mission has not reached a safe terminal state.

This means a mission command must be confirmed through telemetry rather than assumed successful because an API call returned. ArduPilot, MAVLink, and SITL provide the software interfaces used to test these state transitions \cite{ardupilot,dronekitsitl}. The mission queue, target checks, geofence, landing lock, and ordered failsafe actions in this project address these software-side hazards. They do not prove the physical aircraft's behavior in wind, vibration, GPS multipath, or component failure. The literature on personal safety and acoustic recognition generally evaluates sensing or alerting separately from an autonomous aircraft. This project connects the two, which increases the need for conservative authorization. An acoustic candidate cannot directly trigger motors or change flight mode. First, packet authenticity and freshness are checked.

The hub then evaluates the available sound and context. Finally, the flight service checks its own readiness conditions and waits for autopilot confirmation. Each layer can refuse the next transition. This design does not make the system fail-proof, but it makes the basis for a dispatch auditable and prevents one classifier output from becoming a flight command. Evidence recording raises a distinction between a software rehearsal and a validated field capability. A successful SITL mission confirms that the recording window and return sequence can be exercised in simulation, but it does not establish camera coverage, image quality, or performance in outdoor lighting and weather. Those measurements require supervised physical tests. This boundary is important in a safety project: a completed software command sequence is not, by itself, proof of effective evidence capture near the public.

\section{Privacy and public-space acoustic sensing}

Fixed microphones create a privacy question even when the system is intended for emergencies. A system that stores or streams all ambient audio could collect ordinary conversations unrelated to safety. On-device processing, short event-triggered clips, limited retention, and access controls reduce that exposure, but they do not make privacy concerns disappear. Public deployment would also require clear notice, a defined purpose, a lawful basis, a retention and deletion schedule, and an accountable operator. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 is part of the Indian legal context considered in this thesis \cite{dpdp}. The design's privacy objective is data minimization: the node processes ordinary frames locally and does not intentionally store or stream them. A short verification clip may be created after a candidate event and should have a documented retention period.

Alert logs should preserve enough metadata to audit system behavior without turning into a permanent record of ambient sound. Access to recordings and incident details should be limited to the personnel responsible for response and review. The prototype's software configuration and a future production policy must be distinguished. the latter still needs formal review before deployment. Privacy design also interacts with evaluation. Researchers need enough recorded data to measure false alarms and missed events, yet collecting continuous audio solely for convenience conflicts with the minimization objective. A field study can instead define a reviewed collection protocol, obtain the necessary institutional approvals, restrict access, and retain only the samples required for analysis. Data handling must be designed before recording begins rather than added after the system is already in operation.

This makes privacy a system requirement alongside latency, security, and flight safety, not a closing paragraph appended after the hardware design.

\section{Comparative synthesis and research gap}

The reviewed work supplies evidence for separate parts of an emergency response chain. Personal applications and wearables show how a user can initiate an alert and share a location, but they depend on a device carried by the person \cite{pavithra,bharathi,snehith,hadkar,uganya,potturi}. Acoustic research shows that screams and other danger sounds can be classified, and that temporal models can locate a scream interval in a recording \cite{kimwindow,kimscream,sharma,srimathi,fime}. Edge-inference work supports moving part of the classification closer to the microphone \cite{tflm,ciaburro}; emergency-communications research supports low-rate radio where cellular access is unreliable \cite{sciullo,lorawan}, and flight-control tooling makes it possible to exercise mission logic in simulation \cite{ardupilot,dronekitsitl}. These findings do not imply that the parts are interchangeable or that their published performance transfers directly to a new deployment.

Each paper uses a different dataset, hardware platform, radio configuration, mission policy, and success measure. A classifier's benchmark does not measure radio reliability, an alerting prototype does not establish aircraft safety, and a simulated mission does not measure real endurance. The value of the survey is to identify which design decisions have precedent and which claims still need project-specific testing. The gap addressed here is therefore an integration and verification gap. VanniKawachh places the initial microphone at a fixed location, screens sound locally, sends a compact authenticated alert separately from the audio, verifies the event at a hub, and only then requests a drone mission through a state-checked control service. The project contribution is this connected workflow and its software-level evaluation.

It is not a claim that the prototype has already established public-space detection performance or safe autonomous operation in the field. The following chapters describe the architecture and implementation in sufficient detail to separate what has been built and tested from what remains to be measured.

\section{Design decisions and rationale}
\label{sec:rationale}

\textbf{Fixed nodes carry no live GPS.} Each pole is surveyed at
installation using a GPS receiver or phone. The hub stores the node
identifier, coordinates and location name. The alert needs only two
bytes for the identifier instead of carrying a coordinate pair. This
reduces packet size. The hub rejects an identifier that is absent from
its registry.

\textbf{LoRa carries the alert; WiFi carries the audio.} A compact
alert is suited to LoRa's low data rate. A four-second verification
clip needs a faster local link, so the intended design sends the alert
over LoRa and the clip over WiFi or ESP-NOW. Range, delivery rate and
clip latency still need measurement at an installation site.

The split is a direct consequence of LoRa's physical layer. The
duration of one LoRa symbol at spreading factor SF over bandwidth BW
is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:symtime}
T_s = \frac{2^{\mathrm{SF}}}{\mathrm{BW}}
\end{equation}
so higher spreading factors buy sensitivity at the price of airtime.
The receiver's minimum detectable signal is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sens}
P_{\min}\ [\mathrm{dBm}] = -174 + 10\log_{10}(\mathrm{BW}) +
\mathrm{NF} + \mathrm{SNR}_{\min}
\end{equation}
where NF is the receiver noise figure and $\mathrm{SNR}_{\min}$ the
demodulation threshold for the chosen SF.
The proposed 25-byte alert needs much less airtime than a four-second,
16~kHz, 16-bit audio clip of roughly 128~kB. Sending the clip over the
same low-rate radio would introduce a large delay. This is the reason
for separate alert and audio links; it does not establish actual radio
coverage or clip delivery time.

If the clip does not arrive within 8~s, the hub logs the candidate
without a dispatch request. A Stage-1 score alone cannot replace
independent audio verification. Multi-node corroboration
slots into the same fallback point as future work.

\textbf{Screen first, verify before dispatch.} The node sends a
candidate for review; the hub independently checks the audio and
available context. Stage 1 can miss events, as its measured scream
recall shows, and Stage 2 can also make errors. The two-stage design
limits direct dispatch from one classifier score while leaving both
false alarms and missed events for field evaluation.

\textbf{Authenticate each alert.} A forged packet must not enter the
dispatch chain as a trusted event. Section~\ref{sec:packetsecurity}
describes the packet encryption, authentication and replay check.

\textbf{Flight checks remain separate.} The earlier flight software
provides confirmed mode setting, failsafe handling, landing interlocks,
geofence checks and stalled-route detection. These checks form a
software baseline; Chapter~\ref{ch:response} describes their scope
and the evidence still needed for physical operation.

\section{The mission lifecycle (response tier)}
\label{sec:lifecycle}

The response sequence runs from IDLE through CONNECTING, WAITING GPS,
ARMING, TAKEOFF, ENROUTE, and HOVERING, then returns through RTL and
LANDED to COMPLETED. ABORTED and FAILED are abnormal terminal
outcomes. Three earlier
properties are preserved: no state waits for human input; every
transition is timestamped into the mission log; every blocking loop
polls both the failsafe arbiter and the operator-cancel flag, so
abnormal termination is reachable from the active flight phases. The
hover window is the intended evidence-recording interval.

\section{Audio data, development tools, and computing platform}
\label{sec:data-platform}

The Stage-1 training record groups background, scream, cry, and
emergency-word clips into a four-class dataset. Audio is converted to
a consistent sample rate and feature shape before training. Its
reported evaluation contains 1,719 windows, but the split is by
source file rather than by speaker. The checked-in metrics do not
include a complete source manifest, command-line invocation, and
hardware run log. This limits reproducibility and can make results
optimistic when nearby files share a speaker or acoustic setting.
The report therefore gives both the overall 86.62\% result and the
weaker class result: scream recall is 55.52\%, while background
false positives are 7.43\% in that evaluation.

A separate hub-side feature classifier uses a YAMNet TFLite
representation and a speaker-disjoint ASVP-ESD holdout. Its
218-example internal result is 97.25\% accuracy with four false
positives and two false negatives. ASVP-ESD also contributed training
examples, so this is not a source-external test. These two evaluations
answer different questions and cannot be averaged or presented as a
single system accuracy. The run records do not measure live outdoor
precision, inference latency on the sensing board, or the
false-alarm rate of the KY-037 bench route.

Development uses Python tools for dataset preparation, training,
hub services, and ArduPilot simulation. The intended node executes
on an ESP32-S3; the locality hub targets a Raspberry Pi~5; Pixhawk
owns flight stabilization and motor control. The phone and Pi USB
microphone are current alternate audio inputs, not evidence that the
intended pole node is running its model in the field.

\section{Sensing-node firmware}
\label{sec:nodefirmware}

The sensing node is a single Arduino sketch for the
ESP32-S3~\cite{esp32s3} structured as the pipeline of
Section~\ref{sec:threetiers}: I2S capture from the INMP441~\cite{inmp441} at
16~kHz mono; framing and MFCC feature extraction; the TFLite-Micro
Stage-1 model hook; PIR and LDR sampling; AES-sealed LoRa alert
transmission (SX1278, LoRa library by Sandeep Mistry); and the
ESP-NOW/WiFi clip upload to the hub's clip server. The alert carries
the event class (1 = scream, 2 = call for help, 3 = cry, 4 = crash),
the Stage-1 confidence quantized to a byte, the PIR flag, the raw LDR
level, the node battery percentage, and the monotonic packet counter.
Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the planned two-stage pipeline. The
offline Stage-1 model export and firmware interface do not by
themselves establish measured on-board inference.

\textbf{The Stage-1 mathematics.} The feature front-end is the
classical MFCC chain. Each incoming frame is first pre-emphasized to
flatten the spectral tilt of speech,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:preemph}
y[n] = x[n] - 0.97\,x[n-1]
\end{equation}
then windowed with a Hamming window of length $N$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hamming}
w[n] = 0.54 - 0.46\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi n}{N-1}\right)
\end{equation}
before the FFT. The power spectrum is pooled by a bank of $M$
triangular filters spaced uniformly on the mel scale,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mel}
m = 2595\,\log_{10}\!\left(1 + \frac{f}{700}\right)
\end{equation}
and the log energy of each filter output is taken,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fbank}
e_j = \log\!\left(\sum_{k} H_j(k)\,\lvert X(k)\rvert^2\right),
\qquad j = 1,\dots,M
\end{equation}
where $H_j$ is the $j$-th triangular filter. A DCT-II decorrelates the
log energies into the cepstral coefficients
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dct}
c_i = \sum_{j=1}^{M} e_j
\cos\!\left[\,i\left(j - \tfrac{1}{2}\right)\frac{\pi}{M}\right],
\qquad i = 0,\dots,12
\end{equation}
of which the first \textbf{13} are retained as the frame's feature
vector.

The classifier head is a small CNN ending in a softmax over the $K$
event classes,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:softmax}
p_k = \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} e^{z_j}}
\end{equation}
trained (Phase~1) by minimizing the cross-entropy loss
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:xent}
\mathcal{L} = -\sum_{k=1}^{K} y_k \log p_k
\end{equation}
against one-hot labels $y$. For deployment under TensorFlow Lite Micro
the weights and activations are quantized to int8 with the affine map
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:quant}
x_q = \mathrm{round}\!\left(\frac{x}{s}\right) + z
\end{equation}
where $s$ is the per-tensor scale and $z$ the zero point. This step
is intended to reduce memory use. The $<$50~ms frame deadline is
a design budget, not a target-board timing result.

\textbf{Model and deployment boundary.} A trained four-class Stage-1
model (background, scream, cry and help) has been exported as an int8
TensorFlow Lite artifact of 34,920 bytes. On the held-out set described
in Chapter~\ref{ch:testing}, it achieved 86.62\% accuracy, macro-F1
0.79 and weighted F1 0.86. The result supports the trained model and
preprocessing pipeline; it is not a measurement of microphone distance,
weather resistance, node power draw or real-street false alarms. The
firmware retains a separate model-enabled build setting
so a physical-node test cannot silently substitute a heuristic result
for a model result.

The bench ESP32-S3 path is distinct. It uses a KY-037 analogue sound
sensor and sends a signed sound-level candidate over local Wi-Fi to
the Pi. Quiet-room false alarms have been observed, and the candidate
does not directly authorize physical flight. A later sketch captures
a short PCM clip so the Pi can attempt independent verification, but
the documented hardware handoff says this version still needs upload
and testing. Its outputs should not be labelled as the INMP441
classifier's measured performance.

\section{Authenticated field relay and telemetry gate}
\label{sec:field-relay}

The October field-development implementation adds an explicit boundary
between a remote incident report and the physical aircraft. The cloud
edge relay stores signed reports and exposes separate operator,
Pi/ESP-device and read-only diagnostic routes. The local receiver
 or ESP32 flight-relay
firmware claims only an operator-approved, short-lived command. It
then reads the local flight API or MAVLink telemetry before any trigger
request is created. The browser cannot address MAVLink, arm a vehicle
or write a motor output.

The preflight policy rejects a command when the mission queue is busy,
the aircraft is armed, heartbeat or telemetry is stale, GPS lacks a
fresh 3D fix with at least eight satellites, the reported home is not
near the aircraft, RC readiness is absent, or the target violates the
configured range/geofence limits. In its normal form it also requires
known battery-cell count, plausible voltage and at least 30\% reported
battery. The firmware requests and decodes HEARTBEAT,
position, GPS quality, system status, pilot receiver, home position,
armed state and estimator health reports; the
values are displayed as evidence, not guessed from a successful HTTP
request.

The temporary no-battery profile is deliberately narrower than normal
operation. It exists because battery telemetry was unavailable on the
development platform and must be enabled explicitly. It retains GPS,
home, RC and disarmed-state checks, caps API mission duration at
120~s, nominal requested altitude at 1~m, nominal requested speed at
0.5~m/s and the API geofence at 60~m; it omits the observation hover.
It does not establish that flying without battery monitoring is
safe, and it must be removed once a calibrated power module is
available.

\section{Alert authentication and replay protection}
\label{sec:packetsecurity}

The threat model is simple: a spoofed packet launches a drone; an
eavesdropped packet reveals an incident in progress; a replayed packet
re-launches a drone at an attacker's chosen time. The wire format
(25 bytes, which fits in one LoRa frame; Figure~\ref{fig:packet},
full layout in Appendix~\ref{app:packet}) answers all three:

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[font=\small,
  field/.style={draw,align=center,text width=3.45cm,
    minimum height=22mm,inner sep=4pt}]
\node[field,fill=gray!8] at (-4.2,0)
  {\textbf{Header: 9 bytes}\\magic, version, node ID, counter};
\node[field,fill=blue!8] at (0,0)
  {\textbf{Ciphertext: 8 bytes}\\event and context data};
\node[field,fill=red!7] at (4.2,0)
  {\textbf{Tag: 8 bytes}\\truncated HMAC-SHA256};
\end{tikzpicture}
\caption{The sealed alert packet. The header is cleartext; the
event data is encrypted and the packet is authenticated.}
\label{fig:packet}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Confidentiality:} the 8-byte payload (event, confidence,
  PIR, light, battery) is AES-128-CTR encrypted. The CTR nonce is
  derived from the cleartext header (magic, version, node identifier,
  counter), unique per packet as long as the counter is monotonic.
\item \textbf{Authenticity:} an 8-byte MAC (HMAC-SHA256 over header
  + ciphertext, truncated) using the per-node key. A bad MAC is
  dropped before decryption is trusted.
\item \textbf{Replay protection:} the uint32 counter is monotonic per
  node; the hub rejects any counter $\leq$ the last accepted value for
  that node.
\item \textbf{Key management:} each node uses a distinct key derived
  from the hub's master key and its registered identifier. The hub
  holds the master key; the node is provisioned with its derived key.
\end{itemize}

The node identifier and counter travel in cleartext so the hub can
select the key and reject replayed alerts. Both are covered by the
authentication tag.

Formally, the construction is as follows. Node $n$'s key is derived
from the hub's master key $K_m$ as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:keyderive}
K_n = \operatorname{HMAC\mbox{-}SHA256}(K_m,\operatorname{ID}(n))_{0:15}
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{ID}(n)$ denotes the byte string consisting of
``node:'' followed by the node number in decimal, and the subscript
$0{:}15$ selects the first 16 result bytes. The hub holds one master
secret and each node uses its own derived key (HMAC per
RFC~2104~\cite{rfc2104}). The 8-byte payload $P$ is
encrypted in counter mode,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ctr}
C = P \oplus E_{K_n}(\mathrm{IV})
\end{equation}
where $E$ is AES-128~\cite{fips197} and the initial counter block IV
is built from the cleartext header (magic, version, node identifier,
counter). The IV is unique per packet as long as the node's counter
is monotonic. The authentication tag is an encrypt-then-MAC over
everything on the wire,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mac}
\mathrm{tag} = \mathrm{HMAC\mbox{-}SHA256}\!\left(K_n,\
\mathrm{header} \parallel C\right)[0{:}8]
\end{equation}
and the hub accepts a packet if and only if
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:accept}
\mathrm{tag\ valid}\ \wedge\ \mathrm{counter} >
\mathrm{counter}_{\mathrm{last}}
\end{equation}
for that node, bumping $\mathrm{counter}_{\mathrm{last}}$ only after
acceptance. The truncated 8-byte tag leaves a blind-forgery success
probability of $2^{-64}$ per attempt, which is negligible at LoRa
alert rates; the trade-off would be revisited if the channel were
ever widened.

\section{Stage-2 audio verification}
\label{sec:stage2}

The current verifier returns a score and a separate confirmation
decision. It examines overlapping one-second audio windows, spaced
0.25~s apart. Backend selection depends on model availability:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{PANNs.} If its checkpoint loads, the default window
  threshold is 0.70 and at least three positive windows are required.
  This backend is intended for the Pi or a local installation.
\item \textbf{YAMNet.} If PANNs is unavailable, the committed TFLite
  backend uses a default window threshold of 0.30 and three positive
  windows. A single very strong YAMNet window at or above 0.75 can
  also confirm a short event. These are software defaults rather
  than validated field settings.
\item \textbf{Eligible prosodic check.} After the learned backend
  fails to confirm an eligible vocal event, an independent stress
  check may run for an exact emergency-word event or a vocal Stage-1
  candidate with confidence at least 0.75. Its threshold is 0.55.
\item \textbf{Energy heuristic.} When neither learned backend loads,
  a development scorer can still report loudness and spectral
  features. The current verifier marks this result unconfirmed, so
  energy alone cannot dispatch. The earlier Phase-0 rehearsal used
  this fallback under its historical software settings.
\end{itemize}

For completeness, the historical fallback scorer is defined here.
Over the clip's samples $x[n]$, $n = 1,\dots,N$, it
computes the loudness as the root-mean-square level
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rms}
\mathrm{RMS} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} x[n]^2}
\end{equation}
and the spectral centroid (the ``brightness'' of the clip, high for
screams)
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:centroid}
C = \frac{\sum_{k} f_k\,\lvert X(k)\rvert}{\sum_{k}\lvert X(k)\rvert}
\end{equation}
where $X(k)$ is the magnitude spectrum at frequency $f_k$. Together
with a burstiness term ($\mathrm{burst} \in [0,1]$, from the
envelope's peak-to-mean structure) the score is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fallbackscore}
\mathrm{score} = 0.45\,\min\!\left(1, \frac{\mathrm{RMS}}{0.15}\right)
+ 0.35\,\mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{C - 400}{1600},\,0,\,1\right)
+ 0.20\,\mathrm{burst}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ full loudness credit at RMS $\geq 0.15$ full scale, and centroid
credit ramping linearly from 400~Hz to 2~kHz.

\section{Evidence fusion}
\label{sec:fusion}

A night-time scream in a dark spot with motion nearby carries more
weight than a daytime shout on a busy road. With $a$ the Stage-2 audio
score, $c$ the Stage-1 confidence, $p \in \{0,1\}$ the PIR motion
flag, $L \in [0,255]$ the LDR light level, and $n \in \{0,1\}$ the
night indicator (1 between 20:00 and 06:00), the fused severity is the
weighted sum (Figure~\ref{fig:fusionfig})
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fusioneq}
S = 0.60\,a + 0.15\,c + 0.10\,p + 0.08\,d + 0.07\,n,
\qquad d = 1 - \frac{L}{255}
\end{equation}
clamped to $[0,1]$. Audio dominates by design; the context terms
adjust the result. For the automatic audio route, the current hub
requires a confirmed Stage-2 result and the severity gate:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gate}
\mathrm{dispatch\ request} \iff
\mathrm{Stage\mbox{-}2\ confirmed}\ \wedge\ S \ge 0.60
\end{equation}
The verifier applies its model-specific window threshold before
returning confirmation. An accepted service request does not prove
physical takeoff. A supervised operator test has a separate
authorization path and still requires flight-readiness checks.
Mission priority is set separately:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:priority}
\mathrm{priority} = \mathtt{high} \iff S \ge 0.75\ \vee\
\left(a \ge 0.6\ \wedge\ p = 1\right)
\end{equation}
otherwise \texttt{normal}.

\section{Verified mode transition}
\label{sec:verifiedmode}

Every flight-mode change passes through one routine. It sends the
requested mode, then checks the mode reported in the autopilot's
heartbeat until the deadline. If the mode is still unconfirmed after
700~ms, the routine retries using the standard MAVLink command and the
legacy mode command. Success means that the autopilot reports the
requested mode, not merely that the client accepted the request.

If return-to-launch cannot be confirmed during an abort, the routine
attempts landing; it also attempts return-to-launch when landing cannot
be confirmed. This is a recovery attempt, not a guarantee of a safe
landing. The check was motivated by an earlier ArduCopter 3.3 simulator
test: the client reported success when a guided-mode request had not
actually changed the vehicle's mode. The flight service therefore uses
the same confirmed routine for normal and emergency mode changes.

\section{The landing interlock}
\label{sec:interlock}

Two mechanisms, redundant by intent (Figure~\ref{fig:interlock}). The
\textbf{abort guarantee}: every abnormal termination (failsafe,
recall, exception with an airborne vehicle) commands its action
through Section~\ref{sec:verifiedmode} and then blocks, polling armed
state and relative altitude, until disarm or a 240~s bound, before
the queue regains control. The \textbf{pre-flight check}: a dequeued
mission re-reads the armed flag and waits (bounded, 120~s) or fails
rather than arm an armed vehicle. Together they make the invariant
(the queue can never start a flight against an airborne
vehicle) guaranteed by the design, not left to chance. The same
property is
defended at process boundaries: shutdown with an armed vehicle
commands RTL (verified) before the MAVLink link drops (H9).

\section{Failsafe arbitration}
\label{sec:arbiter}

The arbiter evaluates four condition families at 1~Hz (battery low
(20\%) and critical (10\%), GPS fix validity, geofence distance,
mission wall-clock) and maintains exactly one demanded action under
three rules. \textbf{Monotone severity:} demands only escalate (NONE
$<$ RTL $<$ LAND); H6 closed by construction. \textbf{Debounce:} GPS
loss fires only after $N$ (default 3) consecutive bad samples, reset
on recovery; it demands LAND because a return path is non-navigable
without positioning; H4 closed with detection latency bounded at $N$
seconds. \textbf{Fire-once:} each named hazard emits once per
mission, with re-emission permitted only as escalation, so the
incident log is not flooded with repeats. The executor couples to the
arbiter inside every blocking
loop, including the RTL loop, which re-reads the demand each
second and swaps to LAND on escalation (H5).

Two pieces of mathematics underpin the arbiter's inputs. The geofence
evaluator measures the vehicle's great-circle distance from home with
the haversine formula: for latitudes $\varphi_1, \varphi_2$ and
longitude difference $\Delta\lambda$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:haversine}
d = 2R \arcsin\sqrt{\sin^2\!\frac{\Delta\varphi}{2} +
\cos\varphi_1 \cos\varphi_2 \sin^2\!\frac{\Delta\lambda}{2}},
\qquad R = 6371\ \mathrm{km}
\end{equation}
This is the same computation used by edge validation (H7) and by the
acceptance harness's closest-approach metric. The GPS-fix validity the
arbiter debounces is itself the output of the autopilot's extended
Kalman filter, which maintains the state estimate $\hat{x}$ through
the standard predict/update pair
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ekfpredict}
\hat{x}_{k|k-1} = F_k\,\hat{x}_{k-1|k-1}, \qquad
P_{k|k-1} = F_k P_{k-1|k-1} F_k^{\top} + Q_k
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ekfupdate}
\hat{x}_{k|k} = \hat{x}_{k|k-1} +
K_k\left(z_k - H_k\,\hat{x}_{k|k-1}\right),
\qquad
K_k = P_{k|k-1} H_k^{\top}\left(H_k P_{k|k-1} H_k^{\top} +
R_k\right)^{-1}
\end{equation}
fusing GPS, IMU, and barometer. The companion computer deliberately
does not re-implement this estimator; it consumes the autopilot's fix
type and debounces it, trusting the EKF for fusion and itself for
policy.

\section{Hover-phase evidence recording}
\label{sec:camera}

The recorder starts when the mission enters HOVERING and stops before
return-to-launch, writing an mp4 tagged with the mission id into the mission
log directory. This file is the evidence artifact for the incident
record. On
machines without a camera (SITL, dev laptops) it is a no-op stub, so
the mission flow is byte-identical either way; on the airframe it
drives the Pi Camera Module~3. Recording is mission-scoped by
construction: the camera runs only over the incident, never in
transit, and live streaming to police is explicitly future work
(Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}).

\section{What the software refuses to own}
\label{sec:refuses}

Two authorities stay outside the stack by design. The hardware RC
link (pilot mode-switch and kill) overrides anything this software
commands; the last line of defence is hardware, not software. And
ArduPilot's own pre-arm checks and firmware failsafes stay at stock
values on hardware: the SITL-only relaxation is dead code unless
simulation mode, and the documentation treats setting it on a
real aircraft as a defect, not an option.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

The project uses three levels of software validation. Each level
checks a different boundary. Passing a unit test or simulated mission
does not demonstrate safe physical flight or field detection accuracy.

\textbf{Unit tests.} The recorded 68-case inventory consists of 47
flight-control cases, 14 hub cases and 7 routing cases. The flight
tests use a simulated vehicle to exercise failsafe escalation, GPS
debouncing, geofence rejection, queue ordering, cancellation and
landing interlocks. The hub tests check packet authentication, replay
rejection, node lookup, severity fusion and the rule that an incident
below the dispatch gate does not request a mission. The routing tests
check obstacle keep-out geometry. These are software checks; they do
not replace sensor, radio or airframe testing.

\textbf{Simulation acceptance mission.} The earlier flight harness
starts ArduCopter simulation and the flight API, sends a test mission,
and samples telemetry once per second. It checks connection, arming,
takeoff, target approach, return and landing. The target is about
896~m from home, with a requested altitude of 15~m. The recorded
approach and return limits are 5~m and 10~m respectively. Results from
this harness apply to the earlier flight software, not automatically
to the current cloud bridge.

\textbf{Full-chain software rehearsal.} A generated distress clip and
a simulated sensing node exercise the sealed 25-byte alert, clip
matching, hub scoring, fusion and mission request. The drone executes
the mission in simulation and the camera recorder is a no-op. This
rehearsal checks message flow and control sequencing. Its generated
audio, fallback verifier, simulated physics and absent camera footage
limit what can be concluded about a real incident.

\section{Defect case studies (retained from v1)}
\label{sec:defects}

Three defects found during v1 are retained as evidence for the design
philosophy, because they now protect the whole VanniKawachh chain.

\textbf{Case 1: the silent mode rejection (run 1).} State machine
reached TAKEOFF, altitude stayed 0.0~m, vehicle disarmed itself. Log
forensics showed mode still STABILIZE despite the client reporting
GUIDED. Root cause: unverified setter (H1). Fix:
Section~\ref{sec:verifiedmode}. Lesson: the autopilot's report is the
only truth; the client's state is a wish. The two-stage acoustic
pipeline applies the same lesson to Stage-1 claims.

\textbf{Case 2: the racing test predicate (run 2).} The harness
inferred ``connected'' from a telemetry state string and raced a
transition. Fix: an explicit boolean on \texttt{/health}. Lesson:
tests must consume interfaces, not coincidences of internal
state.

\textbf{Case 3: the unguarded abort path (found by review, closed
before it fired).} The original abort used the bare setter (the
exact call proven unreliable in Case 1) and returned with the
vehicle airborne (H2+H3 compound). The unit tier now contains the
regression tests that would have caught both. Lesson: the
emergency path must be held to a higher standard than the nominal
path, and it is the one most easily left to a lower one.

\section{Measurements still needed}
\label{sec:notmeasured}

Stated plainly, because the credibility of the measured numbers
depends on the honesty of the unmeasured ones:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{No field acoustic result exists.} The Stage-1 and
  speaker-disjoint hub results in Section~\ref{sec:acoustic-results} are
  controlled dataset evaluations. The Stage-1 split is by source file
  and lacks a complete run manifest. Real microphone distance, latency,
  power draw, rain/wind/noise robustness and full-pipeline false-alarm
  rate remain unmeasured. The $<$50~ms Stage-1 value remains a design
  budget until it is measured on the exact ESP32-S3 hardware.
\item \textbf{The present KY-037 bench route has false candidates.}
  Quiet-room false alarms were observed. A newer clip-sending sketch
  still needs upload and hardware validation. These observations are
  not results for the intended INMP441 model.
\item \textbf{No external Stage-2 benchmark or field precision exists.}
  The internal 97.25\% result uses a speaker-disjoint ASVP-ESD split;
  it must not be generalized to other voices, languages, streets or
  microphones. The Phase-0 score still used the labelled energy
  heuristic fallback for an integration test, not a measured field
  verifier.
\item \textbf{No radio range or loss figures exist.} LoRa range
  (urban/open) and packet loss vs.\ spreading factor, and ESP-NOW
  clip-delivery reliability, are Phase-2 measurements.
\item \textbf{No autonomous mission through the current cloud bridge
  is documented.} The Pi service is locked in the repository handoff,
  and its bench telemetry lacks usable GPS and battery data. The
  alternative ESP bridge is built but not flashed and does not arm
  or start AUTO. Physical aircraft photographs and earlier SITL runs
  do not establish a current node-to-aircraft mission. A staged path---props-off
  checks, power-module calibration, manual hover, supervised guided
  flight with RC override, then an outdoor VLOS integration test---is
  required before any operational claim.
\end{itemize}

These are the numbers the Phase 1 and 2 bench campaigns exist to produce,
and the papers derived from this thesis will carry them only once
measured.

\section{Safety, Privacy, and Legal Compliance}
\label{ch:compliance}

\subsection{Privacy by construction}
\label{sec:privacy}
The intended fixed node processes ordinary audio frames locally and
sends a short clip only after a candidate trigger. This is a
data-minimization objective, not proof that the whole prototype avoids
collecting personal information. The repository also includes phone
and USB-microphone routes that upload WAV clips to a cloud service.
The encrypted LoRa alert and the audio-clip path are separate; the
repository does not demonstrate end-to-end encryption for every audio
route. Recorded speech can contain sensitive information.

The cloud prototype uses SQLite for incident and command state, with
deployment notes describing ephemeral storage. It does not yet
establish a production retention schedule, deletion process, public
notice, access-control policy, or dependable emergency record. A
public-space study would need an approved collection protocol and a
documented privacy review. The drone camera has a hover-scoped
recording window in software, but usable footage from the reported
simulation was not produced because its recorder was a no-op.

\subsection{Alert integrity and remaining threats}
\label{sec:integrity}
The planned fixed-node LoRa packet uses a per-node key, a truncated
HMAC tag, and a monotonic counter. The hub rejects malformed packets,
unknown nodes, invalid tags, and stale counters before treating a
packet as a fresh candidate. This narrows packet forgery and replay
risks. It does not establish that a microphone heard a real emergency,
that a captured node cannot be abused, or that radio jamming is
prevented. A candidate still requires independent audio confirmation
before automatic dispatch. Signed cloud reports and local telemetry
gates add distinct checks on the newer development path.

\subsection{Flight operations and pilot control}
\label{sec:flightlaw}
India's Drone Rules, 2021 require the intended flight area and its
restrictions to be checked on Digital Sky before operation
\cite{dronerules}. Any outdoor trial in this project is a supervised
visual-line-of-sight test with an RC pilot able to take over. The
repository's physical Pi service remains locked, and its recorded
bench telemetry is insufficient for a guided mission. Registration,
airspace authorization, pilot qualification, aircraft condition, and
local permissions must be verified for the particular test rather
than inferred from a software geofence or a prior simulation.

\subsection{Dispatch restraint}
\label{sec:restraint}
An automatic audio incident requires a Stage-2 confirmation and
sufficient fused severity before reaching the response queue.
The local flight service also checks readiness, including fresh
telemetry, a grounded home, GPS, battery policy, pilot readiness, and
mission state. The separate operator real-mode request is a
short-lived supervised test path; it bypasses audio verification
only, not aircraft safety checks. A queued command, an API
acknowledgement, and a flown mission are different evidence levels.

\subsection{Radio-spectrum compliance}
\label{sec:spectrum}
The intended LoRa installation requires a frequency, equipment,
power, and duty-cycle plan consistent with the applicable Indian
short-range-device rules. The cited WPC notification addresses
low-power equipment in the 865--868~MHz band \cite{wpc}; the
433~MHz SX1278 prototype radio cannot be presumed compliant under
that notification. Before any public installation, the exact
transceiver and antenna configuration must be checked against
current WPC conditions and equipment approvals. The thesis reports
no measured range, packet-loss rate, or completed outdoor radio
deployment.

\collegechapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

VanniKawachh integrates an infrastructure-based safety chain: local
audio screening, authenticated event reporting, hub/relay verification,
and a Pixhawk-controlled response platform. This thesis documents the
whole path from the microphone and MFCC--CNN model to the LoRa packet,
Raspberry Pi and ESP relay, MAVLink safety gate, F450 propulsion system
and mission recovery state machine. Its essential engineering rule is
that a claim is not a confirmation: a Stage-1 score is not an
incident; a signed report is not a mission; a sent mode command is not
a confirmed autopilot mode; and a return command is not a completed
mission until landing and disarming are observed.

The evidence is correspondingly separated. Stage~1 reached 86.62\%
accuracy on 1,719 file-split evaluation windows, with only 55.52\%
scream recall. The separate hub-side feature classifier reached
97.25\% accuracy on a 218-example speaker-disjoint internal holdout.
The earlier software stack completed a recorded SITL acceptance run,
while its Phase-0 rehearsal used synthesized audio and an energy
heuristic. Neither model result estimates outdoor performance, and
neither software run proves footage or autonomous operation through
the current cloud bridges. The present KY-037 bench path has produced
quiet-room false alarms, and physical dispatch remains locked in the
documented Pi setup.

The next contribution must be measured integration: an actual
microphone and board, independently checked verification, reliable
transport, usable recording, and a supervised physical mission with
fresh telemetry and RC takeover. Until then, the project is a
well-specified prototype with useful component results, not a
deployed emergency response system.

\collegechapter{Future Scope}
\section{Future work}
\label{sec:futurework}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Field measurement campaigns:} flash and time the
  measured Stage-1 model on the exact ESP32-S3 node; measure outdoor
  detection distance versus SNR, Stage-1 and Stage-2 latency,
  end-to-end false-positive rate on street noise, node energy use, and
  LoRa range/loss versus spreading factor; then tune fusion thresholds
  from those data.
\item \textbf{Live police streaming:} RTSP/WebRTC video from the
  drone to the dashboard over LTE, extending the evidence recorder of
  Section~\ref{sec:camera} into a real-time feed for responding officers.
\item \textbf{OpenCV victim tracking:} on-drone detection and
  framing of the person in distress during hover, keeping the camera
  and the aircraft usefully positioned without operator input.
\item \textbf{TDOA multi-node localization:} with synchronized
  clocks, time-difference-of-arrival across neighbouring nodes
  upgrades ``which pole heard it'' to a position estimate between
  poles, and provides the multi-node corroboration path sketched in
  Section~\ref{sec:rationale}.
\item \textbf{City-scale mesh:} many nodes per hub, many hubs per
  city; hub-to-hub LoRa or wired backhaul; fleet dispatch with
  per-airframe interlocks (the v1 multi-vehicle design carries over).
\item \textbf{Physical-flight readiness and modernization:} calibrate
  the power module and battery monitor, verify Pixhawk GPS/EKF/RC
  failsafes and MAVLink telemetry, complete props-off and manual-hover
  checks with a safety pilot, then conduct a bounded guided VLOS test
  before attempting an integrated response demonstration. In parallel:
  pymavlink~\cite{pymavlink} / MAVSDK migration, ArduPilot 4.x SITL
  validation and fault-injection campaigns.
\end{enumerate}

\chapter{LoRa Alert Packet Wire Format}
\label{app:packet}

Each alert uses 25 bytes, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:wireformat}.
The first 9 bytes identify the sender and event sequence. Eight
encrypted bytes carry the observation, and the final 8 bytes verify
that the packet has not been altered.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Fields in one 25-byte alert packet.}
\label{tab:wireformat}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.18}
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.66\textwidth}|}
\hline
\textbf{Bytes} & \textbf{Length} & \textbf{Purpose} \\
\hline
0--1 & 2 & Fixed packet marker, identifying this alert format. \\\hline
2 & 1 & Format version. \\\hline
3--4 & 2 & Node identifier, used to select the correct security key. \\\hline
5--8 & 4 & Increasing packet counter, used to reject replayed alerts. \\\hline
9--16 & 8 & Encrypted event and sensor values. \\\hline
17--24 & 8 & Authentication tag covering the header and encrypted values. \\\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The encrypted section stores one byte each for the event class,
confidence, motion flag, light level and battery percentage. Three
bytes are reserved. Event values identify scream, call for help, cry
or crash. The hub derives a separate key for each node from its master
key and that node's identifier. It rejects a packet with the wrong
length or version, an unknown node, an invalid authentication tag or
a counter that is not greater than the last accepted value.
